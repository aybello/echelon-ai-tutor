# Class 3 Water Distribution — Source and Editorial Review: Support Systems

**Review date:** 2026-09-22
**Module reviewed:** Support Systems
**Candidate scope:** Questions **2121–2182** (62 candidates)
**Decision:** **Do not approve for publication. All 62 candidates must remain `in_review`.** This review did not modify the candidate JSON, repository content, or any production system.

## Overall assessment

This module is well aligned at a broad topical level with Water Professionals International’s Class III Water Distribution outline. The outline expressly includes chemical-dose adjustment, chemical-feed equipment maintenance, chemical transport/storage/feed, sample collection, residual/pH/temperature analyses, and laboratory-result interpretation.[1] That document is a **content blueprint**, however, not answer-level authority. Every candidate currently cites it as a “topic-map reference only,” so none has a source that substantiates its exact stem, keyed answer, or explanation. That source pattern is not sufficient for publication.

Most conceptual items have a sound operational core. The calculation answers in **2173–2182** are arithmetically correct as written. The principal problems are: imprecise regulatory language in adverse-result scenarios; insufficient distinctions between regulated, process, and method-specific practices; a few statements that can be read as universal when Ontario requirements are system- or procedure-dependent; and distractors that are so irrelevant that they do not measure Class III judgement. The highest-risk items are **2122, 2140, 2145, 2160, 2164, 2171, and 2181**. They require targeted revision and authoritative citations before another review.

Ontario-specific rules should be applied only where the item expressly places the operator in Ontario or the bank is intended to teach Ontario practice. In particular, secondary-disinfection thresholds, sample frequencies, reporting obligations, corrective actions, and watermain return-to-service rules depend on the system type, regulatory status, approval/licence, and facts of the event.[2] [3] The source support below therefore distinguishes general operational principles from Ontario legal duties.

## Ontario technical anchors that must control revisions

For a required microbiological sample in a chlorinated, non-chloraminated Ontario system, the Regulation calls for an immediate concurrent **free** residual at the same location; in a chloraminated system it calls for **combined** residual, subject to the stated exceptions.[2] This directly supports teaching the free/total/combined distinction, but it also means questions must identify the disinfectant regime before using “free residual” as the only correct operational measure.

Ontario’s secondary-disinfection procedure says that, where secondary disinfection is required, the distribution system must maintain at all times and locations with daily flow at least **0.05 mg/L free chlorine at pH 8.5 or lower**, **0.05 mg/L chlorine dioxide**, or **0.25 mg/L combined chlorine** for monochloramine. It separately describes 0.2 mg/L free chlorine and 1.0 mg/L combined chlorine as recommended optimum targets, not substitutes for the applicable regulatory requirement.[3] Answers about “low,” “high,” or “suitable” residuals therefore need either a stated local setpoint/operating procedure or conditional wording.

Ontario’s sample guide is especially important for the sampling cluster. It requires laboratory-supplied, appropriate containers; says microbiology bottles contain dechlorinating agent; specifies that pre-charged preservative bottles must not be rinsed, overfilled, or have preservative removed; and treats sample identity, collection date/time, location, and accompanying chain-of-custody information as essential. It also specifies a 48-hour maximum holding time and cool storage for total-coliform/*E. coli* samples, while directing collectors to the laboratory for method-specific instructions.[4] The guide supports the instructional intent of the sampling items, but it does **not** license a generic “always disinfect and flush the tap” sequence across every sample type and method.

## High-risk items requiring revision before any publication decision

### 2122 — Tap disinfection and immediate collection

The keyed concern is directionally reasonable, but “tap disinfectant may contaminate the sample unless the procedure’s flush is completed” is too imprecise. A sample can be invalid because collection did not follow the laboratory/method procedure; it is not necessarily accurate to characterize the disinfectant as generic sample “contamination.” The Ontario guidance emphasizes appropriate tap selection, laboratory instructions, preservation, and sample integrity rather than a universal single flush sequence.[4]

**Correction direction:** Rewrite the stem to name a **microbiological sample collected under a procedure that requires tap preparation and flushing**. Key an answer such as: “The collection did not follow the approved laboratory/method procedure, so the sample may not be representative; follow the procedure and recollect if necessary.” Avoid a universal timing rule unless the cited method supplies it. Replace a frivolous distractor with “Accept it because the bottle contains neutralizer,” which tests the distinction between neutralizing residual in the bottle and validating tap preparation.

### 2140 — *E. coli* result

The explanation calls *E. coli* a “pathogen indicator.” It should be called a **fecal indicator organism** in this regulatory context. More importantly, “follow the adverse-result response and notification procedure while verifying” can be read as waiting to verify before acting. In Ontario, *E. coli* is not detectable under the microbiological standard and an applicable adverse result triggers statutory reporting/corrective-action duties.[9] [10]

**Correction direction:** Key the immediate duty: “Immediately make the required notifications and take the prescribed corrective action under the applicable adverse-result procedure; collect follow-up samples as directed.” Do not state or imply that a confirmatory result is a prerequisite to the initial response. Cite the applicable O. Reg. 170/03 Schedule 16 and corrective-action section, and retain “applicable” because the legal path depends on system facts.

### 2145 — Repaired-main return to service

The principle that visual clarity does not establish microbiological safety is correct. The item nevertheless overstates a uniform sequence of “disinfection, sampling and authorization” for every repair. Ontario’s watermain procedure applies to stated municipal-residential works and has differing rules for new mains, connections, work length, witnessed sanitary construction, and placement into service.[11]

**Correction direction:** Amend the answer to: “No. Follow the applicable watermain-disinfection procedure, permit/approval conditions, and operating-authority return-to-service authorization; visual clarity alone is not acceptance evidence.” If the intended fact pattern is a municipal-residential connection, explicitly state that context and cite the particular procedure section.

### 2160 — Confirmed adverse result during isolation

The keyed answer is broadly sensible but does not test the immediate statutory priorities. “Public health response, water quality actions and operational isolation records” is broad enough to be guessed and omits notification, prescribed corrective action, and timed resampling.

**Correction direction:** Use a concrete priority question: “Which actions must proceed without waiting for isolation to finish?” Key: “Make required notifications, implement the prescribed corrective-action/medical-officer directions, preserve a factual timeline, and coordinate isolation and sampling.” Make the jurisdiction explicit. Cite O. Reg. 170/03 and the Ontario adverse-result bulletin.[2] [10]

### 2164 — Total-coliform detection with normal residual

The current answer correctly rejects the fallacy that a normal residual invalidates a microbiological result. It is too vague for an Ontario bank: total coliforms are “not detectable” under the Ontario microbiological standard and applicable adverse results require the regulatory response.[9] A normal residual is useful context, but not an exculpatory test.

**Correction direction:** Change the keyed answer to: “Treat the result under the applicable adverse-result and investigation procedure; the concurrent residual is supporting evidence and does not negate a detected total-coliform result.” Specify whether the sample is a regulated distribution sample, since plumbing-only investigations have important exemptions/qualifications.[10]

### 2171 — Persistently low far-end residual

The diagnostic checks are good, but “review next” may delay required action if the result is a qualifying Ontario distribution residual. For applicable chlorinated systems, less than 0.05 mg/L free residual is an adverse threshold under stated conditions; the regulation prescribes flushing and restoration of secondary disinfection, with escalation where the residual cannot quickly be restored.[2] [3]

**Correction direction:** Put verification and response in sequence: “Verify the reading with a valid representative method immediately; if a reportable threshold is confirmed or indicated, make required notifications and initiate the applicable corrective action while reviewing flow path, turnover, analyser validity, and field samples.” Do not teach a diagnostic-only response to a reportable low residual.

### 2181 — Simple CT product

The arithmetic is correct: 0.9 mg/L × 25 min = 22.5 mg·min/L. The explanation partially mitigates the risk by saying it is not a regulatory credit calculation, but the word “effective contact” can still lead candidates to treat any observed residual and clock time as a defensible CT credit. Ontario defines CT around residual concentration and effective contact time under actual variable flow, temperature, and pH, with system-specific credits and conditions.[3]

**Correction direction:** Either retain it as a pure unit-conversion item labelled **“simple concentration–time product; not a regulatory CT determination”**, or give the required conditions and ask a non-credit calculation. Do not use its result to imply pathogen-inactivation compliance. A stronger distractor would use 0.9 ÷ 25 or a unit conversion error, rather than unrelated numerical values.

## Weak-distractor and assessment-design patterns

The module repeatedly uses three choices that are unrelated to drinking-water operations, such as tank paint, customer tariffs, gate paint, meter serial numbers, roof size, pavement, or vehicle fuel totals. This pattern appears throughout the set, including **2121, 2125, 2127–2131, 2132, 2135–2136, 2139, 2141, 2144, 2146–2148, 2151–2157, 2161, 2163, 2165–2167, and 2171**. It makes the answer identifiable from broad domain familiarity rather than the technical decision point, inflates apparent difficulty calibration, and does not distinguish a competent operator from a test-wise candidate.

Each revised question should contain one clearly best action and three **plausible but incorrect** alternatives from the same operational domain. For residual problems, use alternatives such as changing dose before confirming the analyser, taking a single nonrepresentative sample, relying only on plant-outlet residual, or flushing without verifying flow paths. For sampling problems, use alternatives such as using the wrong bottle, skipping required metadata, treating the neutralizer as a substitute for tap preparation, or accepting an exceeded holding time because the bottle stayed cold. For chemical-feed problems, use alternatives such as trusting stroke count without proving delivery, calculating active mass from solution volume without concentration, or changing controller mode without a documented handoff. Distractors must be false for the stated facts, not merely irrelevant.

A second pattern is overuse of vague action phrases: “follow the procedure,” “representative points,” “suitable,” “controlled return,” and “prescribed investigation.” These are appropriate only when the scenario says whose procedure applies and what regulatory/operational context governs it. They should be replaced with specific actions where the law is clear, especially notification, immediate corrective action, concurrent residual testing, resampling, record preservation, and verification of measurement validity. Conversely, where local procedures legitimately vary—tap preparation, laboratory acceptance, chemical storage, recommissioning, and calibration interval—the item should state that the correct response is to follow the approved laboratory, SDS, operating-authority, licence, or manufacturer procedure.

The calculation items have better answer-relevance than many conceptual items, but they should consistently identify whether the answer is **solution volume**, **active chemical mass**, **nominal hydraulic turnover**, **observed residual loss**, or a **non-credit concentration–time product**. This preserves dimensional clarity and prevents incorrect operational inferences from otherwise correct arithmetic.

## Global correction directions before re-review

1. **Keep every candidate in `in_review`; do not publish from the current draft.** Attach answer-level citations rather than repeating WPI’s topic blueprint as the sole source.
2. **Add an Ontario applicability label** to each regulatory item. State whether it is an Ontario regulated drinking-water scenario, a general operator-practice question, or a local procedure question. Do not generalize municipal-residential rules to all facilities.
3. **Correct adverse-result teaching before re-review.** Items 2140, 2160, 2164, and 2171 must prioritize immediate notification/corrective-action obligations when applicable. Use the exact legal distinction between *E. coli*, total coliforms, concurrent residuals, process analysers, regulatory analysers, and plumbing samples.[2] [7] [9] [10]
4. **Make sampling method-specific.** Use laboratory-provided container, preservative, label, cooling, and holding-time directions. Do not teach a universal disinfect/flush routine, temperature criterion, or bottle treatment where the authoritative source requires the method/laboratory procedure.[4] [6]
5. **Separate operational verification from compliance testing.** Online analyser troubleshooting should include a representative sample flow and a valid paired measurement. Whether an analyser is regulatory or process equipment changes recording, alarm, review, and reporting implications.[7] [8]
6. **Replace weak distractors with operationally plausible alternatives.** Preserve one best answer, remove absurd non-water-work answers, and review psychometrics after revision.
7. **Retain arithmetic but add operational labels and boundaries.** Keep 2173–2180 and 2182 after the listed editorial clarifications. Hold 2181 until it is unambiguously a non-credit C×T exercise.
8. **Preserve traceability.** For sample and system records, specify unique identification, factual correction/audit trail, and the applicable retention requirement. Do not teach deletion, silent alteration, or guessed sample identity.[4] [13] [15]

## Final editorial conclusion

The module has a usable technical foundation, particularly in water-age diagnostics, sample-integrity concepts, analyser verification, chemical-feed calculations, and basic residual relationships. It is **not publication-ready** because answer-level authority is missing across the set and several high-consequence regulatory items are too vague or potentially misleading. The next review should begin only after the high-risk stems/explanations are revised, all retained items receive specific primary-source metadata, and distractors are rebuilt to test realistic operator judgement. The `reviewStatus` of questions **2121–2182** must remain **`in_review`** pending that work.
