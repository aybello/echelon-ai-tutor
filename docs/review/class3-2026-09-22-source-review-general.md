# Class 3 Water Distribution — General Module Source and Editorial Review

**Scope:** Candidate questions **2001–2058** in `candidate-250-2026-09-22.json` (58 questions). The requested focus is distribution hydraulics, pressure zones, pumps, storage, valves, and network operations. This is a **source and editorial review only**. No candidate JSON or repository content was changed. All 58 reviewed records remain **`in_review`**.

## Editorial disposition: do not approve for publication

The module has a sound instructional core. Its hydraulic arithmetic is correct, and many operational interpretations are directionally appropriate. It is **not publication-ready**, however, because every candidate cites the same curriculum-outline document rather than answer-level technical or regulatory evidence. The draft also relies on implausible distractors, uses several ambiguous “first check” prompts, and occasionally presents a simplified diagnostic relationship as if it were a conclusive field determination.

The required repair is targeted rather than wholesale. Retain the core concepts and calculation data, then replace the source pattern with claim-level references, revise the high-risk items listed below, and strengthen all distractors. Ontario design guidance supports the concepts of hydraulic analysis, positive pressure, pressure reducing devices, transient analysis, controls, storage, and interconnections; it also explicitly warns that local or regional requirements may be more stringent. It should not be used to convert a design guideline into an unqualified operational rule for every system. [2] [3]

## Review boundaries and source hierarchy

This review treats the WPI document as an **exam-content map**, not as a source that validates individual keys, numerical assumptions, or operating actions. WPI itself describes the document as a guide to essential job tasks and identifies its primary references for this content area as the CSUS *Water Distribution System Operation & Maintenance* text and AWWA WSO materials. The candidates’ `sourceTitle` says “WPI 2025,” while the supplied URL contains a 2026 path and the displayed PDF does not establish that edition in its body. Cite the document by its displayed title, URL, and access date until a controlled edition is confirmed. [1]

Ontario’s *Design Guidelines for Drinking-Water Systems* is the primary Ontario technical anchor used here. It contains applicable discussion of design pressures, transients, pressure-reducing devices, interconnections, water age, pump controls, storage, instrument calibration, and control-system testing. It is guidance, and it expressly recognizes that another approving authority can impose more stringent requirements. O. Reg. 170/03, the municipal drinking-water licence, approvals, orders, local standards, and site procedures govern when a prompt purports to describe a requirement or direct an operational action. [2] [3] [4]

EPA and AWWA sources are used as recognized technical corroboration where Ontario’s public pages are not answer-level operational manuals. EPA’s EPANET documentation substantiates the modeling concepts for pumps, valves, tanks, pressure, water age, energy, and extended-period simulation. EPA’s water-age and finished-storage fact sheets support the distinction between useful indicators and proof, and support sanitary protection of storage components. AWWA’s public standards index confirms the relevant standards families, but the index alone does not supply a detailed operating criterion. [5] [6] [7] [8] [9]

## Global editorial repairs required before a later publication decision

1. **Replace the source field pattern.** Keep WPI as the blueprint reference, but add a concise answer-level source note for each question. Use Ontario design guidance for Ontario design/hydraulic claims, Ontario legislation or the applicable approval/licence for legal requirements, and EPA/EPANET/AWWA for general technical principles. Do not cite a broad standards index as if it proves a numerical limit or procedure. [1] [2] [3] [4] [8] [9]

2. **Separate conceptual hydraulics from field direction.** A stem about an open bypass, pressure-zone intertie, low suction pressure, surge, or isolation should ask what the data indicate or what must be evaluated. It should not read as authorization to change a valve, bypass, setpoint, or pump speed. Add “under the approved operating procedure” where an answer otherwise resembles an instruction.

3. **State the assumptions that make a hydraulic shortcut valid.** For pressure-head items, identify common datum, static/no-flow conditions, vented reservoir conditions, negligible velocity-head difference, and ignored losses as appropriate. For volume-over-flow items, say “nominal hydraulic detention time” or “idealized turnover estimate,” not actual water age.

4. **Repair terminology.** Reserve **cross-connection** for an actual or potential potable/non-potable connection and its backflow risk. Call a pressure-zone link an **intertie**, **bypass**, or **uncontrolled hydraulic connection**. Distinguish **gauge pressure**, **pressure head**, **hydraulic grade line (HGL)**, **total head**, **flow**, **demand**, and **storage change**.

5. **Replace implausible distractors.** Repeated options about paint colour, customer addresses, meter-reader age, map font, tank-ladder length, and account balances are noncompetitive. Each question should have three distractors drawn from realistic errors: confusing static with residual pressure; overlooking a valve/bypass state; using a wrong datum or units; treating a meter difference as a confirmed leak; or selecting a related but incomplete control variable.

6. **Remove unsupported prioritization.** “First check,” “safest immediate basis,” and “most plausible” require a defined operational context, alarm priority, data quality, and safety constraints. Otherwise ask the less prescriptive question: “Which data should be compared?” or “Which hypothesis is supported but not proven?”

7. **Add measurement-quality language.** Where pressure, flow, level, differential pressure, or telemetry is compared, require contemporaneous readings, known tap locations, common datum, instrument calibration/verification, and documented valve/pump configuration. Ontario guidance calls for representative monitoring, calibration procedures, archived status/alarm information, and testing control sequences against the process narrative. [3]

8. **Use jurisdictional caveats for pressure, fire, and interties.** Fire protection, service-pressure limits, interconnection protocols, cross-connection protection, emergency supply, and operational monitoring are system- and jurisdiction-specific. The Ontario design guideline calls for review of local requirements, water-quality mixing implications, and pressure impacts. [3] [4]

9. **Keep the calculation set, but make it assessment-grade.** The ten calculations (2049–2058) calculate correctly. State the given idealizations, specify inside diameter where needed, choose realistic distractors based on unit/area/conversion errors, and do not turn a calculated hydraulic diameter into a universal pipe-design recommendation.

## Weak-distractor pattern and replacement standard

The module repeatedly signals the key through obviously irrelevant distractors. Examples include “tank paint condition” (2013), “hydrant paint colour” (2016 and 2039), “customer addresses” (2017), “meter reading font” (2032), “tank ladder length” (2032), and “account balances” (2039). These choices do not test a plausible misconception, make the item easier than its stated difficulty, and teach candidates that test-wise elimination can replace hydraulic reasoning.

For each revision, use one correct answer and three alternatives that are technically adjacent but wrong for a stated reason. For an HGL question, alternatives can confuse pressure head with elevation head, omit datum, or use total head rather than HGL. For a pump question, alternatives can use discharge pressure alone, nameplate capacity, an unverified check-valve condition, or ignore the system curve. For storage balance, alternatives can omit storage change, treat inlet flow as customer demand, or assume a level trend without checking instruments. Each option must be grammatically parallel, similar in length, free of “always/never” giveaways unless technically justified, and independently defensible as incorrect.

## High-risk items requiring rewrite before any later approval

**2016, 2021, 2022, 2026, 2027, 2032, 2033, 2036, 2039, 2041, 2045, 2055, 2056, and 2058** require a substantive rewrite, not merely a citation replacement. Their present wording risks overclaiming diagnostic certainty, collapsing total-head concepts into pressure differences, misusing cross-connection terminology, or presenting jurisdiction-/site-dependent operating decisions as generic rules.

Questions **2006, 2012, 2014, 2025, 2030, 2040, and 2047** should also be edited before publication because their data-validation, safety, or prioritization wording is too open to interpretation. The remaining questions can proceed only to a later review stage after source replacement, credible distractor revision, and a final technical edit; none is approved by this report.
