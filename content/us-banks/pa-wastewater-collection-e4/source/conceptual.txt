@Collection systems|PADEP23|Infiltration and inflow: definitions, sources, effects, and methods of finding them
In a sanitary sewer, what is infiltration?|Groundwater that seeps in through cracked pipes, leaking joints and manhole walls|Rainwater that enters the sewer directly through a roof drain or a manhole cover|Wastewater that leaks out of a cracked pipe and soaks into the surrounding soil|Water that flows back from the treatment plant into the upstream sewer|Infiltration is groundwater that finds its way into the pipe through openings below the water table. Which of these is a source of inflow rather than infiltration?|A roof downspout connected to a building sewer|A root-damaged joint under a dry hillside slope|A cracked pipe in the bottom of a deep trench|A broken lateral in wet soil near a creek bed|A downspout sends rain straight into the sewer, so the water never passes through soil. How does the timing of inflow usually differ from that of infiltration?|Inflow peaks quickly in a storm and drops soon after, while infiltration changes more slowly|Inflow rises slowly over several weeks of rain, while infiltration peaks during the storm itself|Both peak at the same time and then drop at the same rate once the rain stops|Inflow occurs in the summer months, while infiltration occurs during the winter months|Surface sources respond right away to rainfall. Groundwater takes longer to rise and drain, so infiltration continues after the storm has passed.|m
Why is minimum nighttime flow in dry weather used to estimate infiltration?|Household use is very low at night, so most of the remaining flow is groundwater|Industrial use is highest at night, so the flow is easier to measure in the pipe|Rain is more likely to fall at night, so the recorded flows are at their highest|Pumps run at full speed at night, so the flow readings are the most stable|In the early morning hours, little wastewater is produced. What is dye testing used for in an I/I investigation?|To confirm that a drain is connected to the sewer by watching for dye downstream|To measure how fast groundwater is moving toward a leaking joint in the pipe wall|To color the flow so that cameras can read the pipe wall more clearly|To estimate the age of a pipe by timing how long the dye takes to travel|Dye is added to a suspected source, such as a drain or downspout, and a downstream manhole is checked to see whether the colored water arrives.|m
During storms, the flow in a basin rises sharply and drops back within hours after rain ends, with little change in baseline flow. What source is most likely?|Direct inflow from roof drains or similar surface connections|Seasonal infiltration from a high water table that builds up over weeks|Normal changes in household water use during the working week|Slow leakage from a lateral that runs through a dry area of town|A quick rise and fall that follows rainfall points to direct connections to surface water, not groundwater entering through the soil.|h
