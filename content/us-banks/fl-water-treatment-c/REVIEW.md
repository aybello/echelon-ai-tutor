# Florida Drinking Water Treatment Plant Operator, Class C (DEP): 150-question review

150 original practice questions: 114 conceptual and 36 calculations, in US customary units. DEP's June 2026 handbook lists the Class C drinking-water-treatment subject areas (aeration; source-water characteristics; clarification and sedimentation; coagulation and flocculation; corrosion control; disinfection and disinfectant byproducts; distribution; filtration; facility operation and maintenance; equipment evaluation and operation; iron and manganese control; laboratory sampling, analysis and interpretation; mathematics; safety and security; regulations; reservoir and well-field management; softening; taste and odor control; waste handling and disposal; water process) with no weights and no objectives, so the questions are spread across those areas by judgement and kept at general entry-level practice. No Florida rule numbers, limits or deadlines are tested. Class A, B and D, wastewater and distribution content are excluded. This is the first release size; more questions can be added later at the end of the source files. This is a local content package, not proof of a production import.

Correct answers are shown beneath each item. See README.md for scope, sources and limits.

### 1: Raw water quality and seasonal change (easy)

Which raw water source usually shows the biggest jump in turbidity after a heavy rainstorm?

- **A.** Water from a deep confined aquifer protected by clay layers
- **B.** Water held in a covered finished-water tank
- **C.** Water from a well with a long steel casing
- **D.** A river or stream draining open farmland

**Answer: D. A river or stream draining open farmland**

Surface runoff carries soil and debris into rivers quickly, while deep groundwater is filtered by the ground and changes slowly.

### 2: Raw water quality and seasonal change (easy)

What does turbidity tell an operator about a raw water sample?

- **A.** How well it resists a change in pH when acid is added
- **B.** How much dissolved gas it carries at the sample temperature
- **C.** How cloudy it is from suspended particles
- **D.** How much dissolved calcium and magnesium it holds in solution

**Answer: C. How cloudy it is from suspended particles**

Turbidity is a measure of light scattered by suspended particles such as clay, silt and organisms. Dissolved minerals and buffering are different properties.

### 3: Raw water quality and seasonal change (easy)

In water treatment, what does alkalinity describe?

- **A.** Resistance to a drop in pH when acid is added to it
- **B.** The amount of suspended clay and silt carried by the water
- **C.** The total of dissolved salts that leave scale on heaters
- **D.** The quantity of oxygen available to aquatic life in the source

**Answer: A. Resistance to a drop in pH when acid is added to it**

Alkalinity is the buffering capacity of the water, mostly from bicarbonate, carbonate and hydroxide. It matters because coagulants consume it.

### 4: Raw water quality and seasonal change (medium)

Which pair of dissolved minerals is mainly responsible for the hardness of a water supply?

- **A.** Chloride and sulfate from road salt and rock
- **B.** Calcium and magnesium dissolved from rock
- **C.** Sodium and potassium found in many brines
- **D.** Iron and manganese from deep wells

**Answer: B. Calcium and magnesium dissolved from rock**

Hardness is the sum of the calcium and magnesium concentrations, usually expressed as mg/L as calcium carbonate.

### 5: Raw water quality and seasonal change (medium)

A cold winter raw water supply is treated with the same coagulant dose as in summer. What is the usual effect of the low temperature on settling?

- **A.** Particles settle more slowly because the water is more viscous
- **B.** Particles settle at the same speed because their own weight sets the rate
- **C.** Particles settle faster because the water is denser than in summer
- **D.** Floc forms faster so the basins need less time to do the job

**Answer: A. Particles settle more slowly because the water is more viscous**

Cold water is more viscous, so it resists the fall of particles and slows both floc formation and settling. Plants often adjust dose or mixing in winter.

### 6: Raw water quality and seasonal change (medium)

A river intake receives a large rainfall runoff. Which change in the raw water is most likely?

- **A.** Alkalinity climbs and the color of the water disappears
- **B.** Hardness climbs and the dissolved gases leave the water
- **C.** Turbidity falls and hardness climbs because of dilution of solids
- **D.** Turbidity climbs and alkalinity often drops from dilution

**Answer: D. Turbidity climbs and alkalinity often drops from dilution**

Runoff brings in solids and dilutes the mineral content that gives alkalinity and hardness, so coagulant demand can change quickly.

### 7: Raw water quality and seasonal change (hard)

Why do operators of a deep reservoir intake watch for the lake turning over in autumn?

- **A.** Algae growth stops, so turbidity falls near zero at all depths
- **B.** Cooling surface water strips dissolved minerals out of the lake
- **C.** Bottom water mixes upward and may bring iron, manganese and odors
- **D.** Alkalinity is lost from the water as the layers mix together

**Answer: C. Bottom water mixes upward and may bring iron, manganese and odors**

When temperature layers break down, bottom water rich in dissolved metals and decay products reaches the intake depth, and treatment needs often change.

### 8: Gas transfer and oxidation (easy)

What is the main job of aeration in a drinking water plant?

- **A.** Settling heavy particles out of the water before filtration
- **B.** Moving gases between the water and the air
- **C.** Adding hardness to make the water taste better
- **D.** Leaving a lasting disinfectant residual in the pipes

**Answer: B. Moving gases between the water and the air**

Aeration brings water and air into close contact so gases can be added, such as oxygen, or stripped, such as hydrogen sulfide and carbon dioxide.

### 9: Gas transfer and oxidation (easy)

Raw well water has a rotten-egg smell. Which gas does aeration help remove?

- **A.** Ozone left from earlier treatment
- **B.** Oxygen from the surrounding air
- **C.** Hydrogen sulfide gas from decaying matter
- **D.** Nitrogen that is dissolved from the air

**Answer: C. Hydrogen sulfide gas from decaying matter**

Hydrogen sulfide is a dissolved gas that escapes to the air during aeration, which reduces the odor. Oxygen and nitrogen are gases that normally dissolve into the water.

### 10: Gas transfer and oxidation (medium)

Aeration strips carbon dioxide from a groundwater. What happens to the pH?

- **A.** It rises as carbonic acid is lost from the water
- **B.** It falls because oxygen has been added to the water
- **C.** It falls because alkalinity is removed by the spray
- **D.** It stays the same because air is a neutral gas

**Answer: A. It rises as carbonic acid is lost from the water**

Dissolved carbon dioxide forms a weak acid. When it leaves the water the acid is reduced and the pH goes up.

### 11: Gas transfer and oxidation (medium)

Why does aeration help in removing dissolved iron from a well supply?

- **A.** Air dissolves the iron into a gas that escapes from the water
- **B.** Air disinfects the iron-using bacteria that live in the water
- **C.** Air lowers the pH enough to dissolve the iron deposits
- **D.** Oxygen turns it into solid particles that can be removed

**Answer: D. Oxygen turns it into solid particles that can be removed**

Ferrous iron in solution reacts with oxygen and forms ferric iron compounds that do not dissolve, so they can be removed as particles.

### 12: Gas transfer and oxidation (medium)

Why does a spray aerator break water into small droplets?

- **A.** It cools the water to lower its hardness before treatment
- **B.** It gives more water surface area for faster gas transfer
- **C.** It adds pressure so that gases stay dissolved in the water
- **D.** It slows the water so more sludge can settle out

**Answer: B. It gives more water surface area for faster gas transfer**

Gas moves across the water-air surface, so more surface area per volume means faster transfer.

### 13: Gas transfer and oxidation (medium)

Compared with cold water, what is the oxygen saturation level in warm water?

- **A.** Higher, because warm molecules hold more dissolved gas
- **B.** The same, because air pressure alone sets the value
- **C.** Lower, since gases are less soluble as the water warms up
- **D.** Higher, because warm water is thinner and lets gas in faster

**Answer: C. Lower, since gases are less soluble as the water warms up**

The solubility of gases drops as temperature rises, so warm water holds less dissolved oxygen at saturation.

### 14: Gas transfer and oxidation (hard)

Brown crusty deposits keep building on the trays and nozzles of an aerator treating raw well water. What is the most likely cause?

- **A.** Sludge rising from a clarifier and splashing into the spray
- **B.** Iron and manganese oxidized by the air and left as solids
- **C.** Disinfection byproducts forming as the droplets fall through air
- **D.** Coagulant carried in from an upstream basin and left on the metal

**Answer: B. Iron and manganese oxidized by the air and left as solids**

Dissolved metals in the well water react with oxygen and form solid oxides that stick to surfaces, so aerators need routine cleaning.

### 15: Coagulants, mixing, jar testing and chemistry (easy)

What does a coagulant do to the tiny particles in raw water?

- **A.** Reduces the charge that keeps them apart in the water
- **B.** Kills them so they stop moving through the water
- **C.** Raises their weight by adding heavy minerals to each one
- **D.** Dissolves them so they pass through the filters easily

**Answer: A. Reduces the charge that keeps them apart in the water**

Small particles carry like charges that keep them apart. A coagulant neutralizes this charge so the particles can stick together.

### 16: Coagulants, mixing, jar testing and chemistry (easy)

Why is a coagulant added where the water is mixed very vigorously?

- **A.** It raises the temperature of the water before settling
- **B.** It breaks up the floc that has already formed in the basin
- **C.** It lets sludge settle faster inside the pipe
- **D.** It spreads through the water evenly within seconds

**Answer: D. It spreads through the water evenly within seconds**

The coagulant reacts almost at once, so it must be dispersed quickly and evenly for good particle contact.

### 17: Coagulants, mixing, jar testing and chemistry (easy)

What is the purpose of a jar test?

- **A.** To measure how much water a plant treats in a day
- **B.** To measure the sludge solids sitting in a basin
- **C.** To calibrate a turbidimeter before the day's readings
- **D.** To find a good dose using small samples of raw water

**Answer: D. To find a good dose using small samples of raw water**

A jar test copies mixing and settling on a small scale so an operator can compare doses and pH before changing the plant feed.

### 18: Coagulants, mixing, jar testing and chemistry (medium)

Why is the water stirred slowly during flocculation instead of vigorously?

- **A.** The floc dissolves back into the water at higher speeds
- **B.** Slow stirring makes the particles repel each other more
- **C.** Particles meet and grow without being torn apart
- **D.** Slow stirring raises the coagulant dose needed for treatment

**Answer: C. Particles meet and grow without being torn apart**

Gentle motion brings particles together to build larger floc, while strong shear tears fragile floc into small pieces.

### 19: Coagulants, mixing, jar testing and chemistry (medium)

What does feeding alum do to raw water alkalinity and pH?

- **A.** Both fall because alum is acidic and uses up alkalinity
- **B.** Both rise because alum behaves like a strong base
- **C.** Both stay the same because alum is a neutral salt
- **D.** The pH rises while the alkalinity falls

**Answer: A. Both fall because alum is acidic and uses up alkalinity**

Alum reacts with alkalinity when it forms floc and releases acid, so alkalinity and pH go down.

### 20: Coagulants, mixing, jar testing and chemistry (medium)

Low-alkalinity raw water cannot hold its pH when alum is fed. What is a common correction?

- **A.** Add acid to lower the pH even further for floc growth
- **B.** Add lime or soda ash to supply alkalinity
- **C.** Shut off the rapid mix to save coagulant
- **D.** Increase the flocculator speed to keep floc suspended

**Answer: B. Add lime or soda ash to supply alkalinity**

A base adds the buffering that alum consumes, so the pH stays in a range where floc forms well.

### 21: Coagulants, mixing, jar testing and chemistry (hard)

Four jars are given alum at 10, 20, 30 and 40 mg/L. After settling, the turbidities are 5.2, 2.4, 0.8 and 1.9 NTU. What is the best conclusion?

- **A.** 40 mg/L works best because more coagulant makes more floc
- **B.** 20 mg/L is best because it is cheaper and removes most turbidity
- **C.** 30 mg/L works best, and 40 mg/L looks like an overdose
- **D.** 10 mg/L is best because the dose is the lowest tested

**Answer: C. 30 mg/L works best, and 40 mg/L looks like an overdose**

The lowest settled turbidity shows the best dose. Turbidity rising at the higher dose is a sign of overfeeding, which can restabilize particles.

### 22: Settling, detention time, overflow rate and sludge (easy)

What is the purpose of a sedimentation basin?

- **A.** To add dissolved oxygen to the water before it is stored
- **B.** To let floc settle out by gravity before the filters
- **C.** To raise the alkalinity of the water before filtration
- **D.** To disinfect the water through long contact time

**Answer: B. To let floc settle out by gravity before the filters**

Quiet water with a long path lets suspended solids sink to the floor, which reduces the load on the filters.

### 23: Settling, detention time, overflow rate and sludge (easy)

What does detention time describe for a basin?

- **A.** The time the coagulant takes to dissolve in the feed tank
- **B.** The time needed for a jar test to finish a full run
- **C.** The time between sludge removals from the floor
- **D.** The average time water spends in the basin

**Answer: D. The average time water spends in the basin**

Detention time is basin volume divided by flow. It shows how long water has to settle on average.

### 24: Settling, detention time, overflow rate and sludge (medium)

What is short-circuiting in a sedimentation basin?

- **A.** Part of the flow reaches the outlet faster than the average
- **B.** Sludge collects in a single corner of the floor
- **C.** Water recirculates in the basin and gets extra settling
- **D.** Chemicals react before they reach the basin inlet

**Answer: A. Part of the flow reaches the outlet faster than the average**

Density currents, wind or poor baffles can send part of the flow straight to the outlet, so real settling time is less than calculated.

### 25: Settling, detention time, overflow rate and sludge (medium)

Floc is seen carrying over the effluent weirs of a clarifier. Which cause fits best?

- **A.** The sludge blanket has compacted too much on the floor
- **B.** The water is warmer than the design temperature
- **C.** Flow per foot of weir is too high for the design
- **D.** The detention time is longer than the design value

**Answer: C. Flow per foot of weir is too high for the design**

High weir loading makes strong upward currents near the outlet that can lift light floc into the settled water.

### 26: Settling, detention time, overflow rate and sludge (medium)

Why should settled sludge be removed on a regular schedule?

- **A.** Buildup takes basin volume and can release odors later
- **B.** It lowers the detention time to zero at the weirs
- **C.** It makes the coagulant feed less efficient at night
- **D.** It raises the alkalinity of the water leaving the basin

**Answer: A. Buildup takes basin volume and can release odors later**

Buildup shrinks the working volume, can resuspend solids and can decay and cause problems in the finished water.

### 27: Settling, detention time, overflow rate and sludge (medium)

How do tube or plate settlers improve clarifier performance?

- **A.** They raise the temperature of the water to settle faster
- **B.** They remove sludge by suction at the outlet weir
- **C.** They add oxygen to speed up floc formation in the basin
- **D.** They add settling surface so particles fall a short distance

**Answer: D. They add settling surface so particles fall a short distance**

The shallow channels give particles only a short fall to a surface, so the basin can handle more flow or give better clarity.

### 28: Settling, detention time, overflow rate and sludge (hard)

Flow to a rectangular settling basin doubles and nothing else changes. What happens?

- **A.** Overflow rate doubles and detention time also doubles
- **B.** Overflow rate doubles and fine floc may escape the basin
- **C.** Overflow rate stays the same since the area is unchanged
- **D.** Overflow rate halves and settling improves for small floc

**Answer: B. Overflow rate doubles and fine floc may escape the basin**

Overflow rate is flow divided by surface area, so doubling the flow doubles it, and detention time halves, so finer floc may carry over.

### 29: Rapid sand, backwash, slow sand and membranes (easy)

What is the main job of a rapid sand filter in a surface water plant?

- **A.** Dissolve calcium and magnesium out of the water
- **B.** Leave a chemical residual to protect the pipes
- **C.** Strip dissolved gases from the water before storage
- **D.** Catch fine particles left after settling

**Answer: D. Catch fine particles left after settling**

Granular media traps small floc and particles that escape the sedimentation basin, giving clear water. It does not change hardness, residual or gas content.

### 30: Rapid sand, backwash, slow sand and membranes (easy)

Why is a filter backwashed?

- **A.** To add new media grains to the top of the bed
- **B.** To measure the rate of flow through the bed
- **C.** To wash trapped solids out of the media bed
- **D.** To raise the pH of the water before the clearwell

**Answer: C. To wash trapped solids out of the media bed**

Solids lodged between the grains raise head loss. Reversing the flow lifts and scrubs them away so the filter can run again.

### 31: Rapid sand, backwash, slow sand and membranes (medium)

During a filter run, the head loss across the bed slowly climbs. What is the usual reason?

- **A.** Trapped solids fill the pores in the bed
- **B.** The water gets warmer as it moves down the bed
- **C.** The underdrain openings widen with use
- **D.** The media grains swell as they take up water

**Answer: A. Trapped solids fill the pores in the bed**

As solids collect in the pore spaces, water meets more resistance and more pressure is lost. A rising head loss is a signal that a wash is coming due.

### 32: Rapid sand, backwash, slow sand and membranes (medium)

Why is a filter often run to waste for a short time after a backwash?

- **A.** Chlorine must be rinsed off the media before use
- **B.** Early filtrate may be cloudy, so it is diverted
- **C.** Head loss has to be built up to a working value
- **D.** The wash trough has to drain before the bed is used again

**Answer: B. Early filtrate may be cloudy, so it is diverted**

Right after cleaning, the bed has not yet regained good particle capture, so the early water is sent away instead of to the clearwell.

### 33: Rapid sand, backwash, slow sand and membranes (medium)

What does a slow sand filter depend on for much of its cleaning action?

- **A.** A living biological layer at the top of the sand
- **B.** Coagulant fed directly onto the bed surface
- **C.** A high rate of flow that forces particles through
- **D.** Strong backwash flows that lift the whole bed

**Answer: A. A living biological layer at the top of the sand**

Slow sand filters run at low rates with no chemical feed. A biologically active layer on the surface traps particles and breaks down organisms.

### 34: Rapid sand, backwash, slow sand and membranes (medium)

How do low-pressure membrane filters mainly remove particles from water?

- **A.** By letting them settle in quiet channels
- **B.** By reacting them with a coagulant on the surface
- **C.** By blocking particles larger than the pores
- **D.** By trapping them on a sticky biological film

**Answer: C. By blocking particles larger than the pores**

Membrane filtration works like a physical sieve, and the pore size sets what is blocked. It does not rely on settling, biology or chemical reaction.

### 35: Rapid sand, backwash, slow sand and membranes (hard)

A filter shows rising effluent turbidity soon after it returns to service, yet its head loss is still low. Which cause fits best?

- **A.** Mudballs have formed and blocked the bed
- **B.** Weak floc from poor coagulation slips through
- **C.** The backwash flow was too gentle to lift the bed
- **D.** The filter has reached its limit of head loss

**Answer: B. Weak floc from poor coagulation slips through**

Particles slipping through while head loss stays low point to poor pretreatment that makes weak floc. Blocked media or a spent run would show high head loss.

### 36: Chlorine, contact time, residual and byproducts (easy)

What is a disinfectant residual?

- **B.** The amount of chemical measured out by the feed pump
- **C.** The portion of the dose used up by reacting with impurities
- **D.** The disinfectant still present after the contact period

**Answer: D. The disinfectant still present after the contact period**

Operators measure what is left in the water to confirm protection carries beyond the plant. The portion that gets used up is the demand, not the residual.

### 37: Chlorine, contact time, residual and byproducts (easy)

Chlorine gas escapes from a leak into a room. Where does it tend to collect?

- **A.** In low areas, since it is denser than air
- **B.** Around the windows, since it is drawn to light
- **C.** Near the ceiling, since it is lighter than air
- **D.** Evenly through the room, since gases mix at once

**Answer: A. In low areas, since it is denser than air**

Chlorine gas is about two and a half times as dense as air, so a release settles toward the floor and into low spots.

### 38: Chlorine, contact time, residual and byproducts (medium)

In disinfection, what does the term CT mean?

- **A.** Coagulant turbidity ratio across the filter
- **B.** Residual concentration times contact time
- **C.** Chemical temperature multiplied by water pH
- **D.** Chlorine tank capacity divided by plant flow

**Answer: B. Residual concentration times contact time**

CT combines how much disinfectant is in the water with how long the water stays in contact. A larger CT gives more inactivation of organisms.

### 39: Chlorine, contact time, residual and byproducts (medium)

Why are baffles built into a chlorine contact basin?

- **A.** They warm the water to speed up the reaction
- **B.** They remove ammonia before the water leaves
- **C.** They stop part of the flow rushing through early
- **D.** They raise the chlorine concentration inside the basin

**Answer: C. They stop part of the flow rushing through early**

Baffles force the water along a longer path, so less of it passes through early and the effective contact time moves closer to the calculated time.

### 40: Chlorine, contact time, residual and byproducts (medium)

As pH rises, what happens to the killing power of free chlorine?

- **A.** It strengthens because more hypochlorite ion is present
- **B.** It strengthens because more chlorine gas is released
- **C.** It stays the same because the dose has not changed
- **D.** It weakens because less hypochlorous acid is present

**Answer: D. It weakens because less hypochlorous acid is present**

Hypochlorous acid is the stronger disinfectant form of free chlorine, and it shifts toward the weaker hypochlorite ion as pH goes up.

### 41: Chlorine, contact time, residual and byproducts (medium)

Which step best lowers the formation of trihalomethanes and haloacetic acids?

- **A.** Feeding chlorine at the plant inlet at a higher dose
- **B.** Warming the water in the clearwell
- **C.** Letting treated water sit longer in the contact basin
- **D.** Removing natural organic matter ahead of chlorine

**Answer: D. Removing natural organic matter ahead of chlorine**

These byproducts form when chlorine reacts with natural organic matter, so taking that material out first leaves less to react. More chlorine, time and warmth tend to increase formation.

### 42: Chlorine, contact time, residual and byproducts (hard)

Chlorine is added in rising doses to water that contains ammonia. The residual climbs, dips, then climbs again. What does the dip show?

- **A.** Chlorine is leaving the water as gas and wasting dose
- **B.** Combined chlorine is just starting to form
- **C.** Combined chlorine is largely broken down
- **D.** The demand from the filter media has been met

**Answer: C. Combined chlorine is largely broken down**

At the low point, chloramines are being broken down by the added chlorine. Doses beyond that leave a free chlorine residual that rises with the dose.

### 43: Lime, soda ash and ion exchange softening (easy)

What is the goal of precipitation softening?

- **A.** Swap sodium for hardness minerals on a resin bed
- **B.** Turn hardness minerals into removable solids
- **C.** Add calcium and magnesium to the water for taste
- **D.** Lower the pH so scale dissolves from the pipes

**Answer: B. Turn hardness minerals into removable solids**

Chemicals such as lime change the water chemistry so that hardness minerals form solids, which settle or are filtered out.

### 44: Lime, soda ash and ion exchange softening (easy)

Which is a common sign of very hard water in a home?

- **A.** Scale on heaters and poor soap lather
- **B.** A cloudy look that clears from the bottom up
- **C.** A rotten-egg odor at the hot tap
- **D.** A bitter taste that develops after boiling

**Answer: A. Scale on heaters and poor soap lather**

Calcium and magnesium leave scale when water is heated and react with soap, which cuts the lather. The other signs point to hydrogen sulfide or trapped air.

### 45: Lime, soda ash and ion exchange softening (medium)

Which kind of hardness needs soda ash in addition to lime?

- **A.** Carbonate hardness, which lime alone can remove
- **B.** Hardness that disappears when the water is boiled
- **C.** Noncarbonate hardness, not tied to alkalinity
- **D.** Hardness caused by sodium in the raw water

**Answer: C. Noncarbonate hardness, not tied to alkalinity**

Lime removes carbonate hardness. Soda ash supplies carbonate that precipitates the calcium left behind as noncarbonate hardness.

### 46: Lime, soda ash and ion exchange softening (medium)

In a cation exchange softener, what happens as hard water passes through the resin?

- **A.** Hardness ions are traded for sodium on the resin
- **B.** Calcium and magnesium form solids that settle on top
- **C.** The resin adds hydroxide that lifts the pH close to 11
- **D.** The resin takes up the sodium and leaves the hardness

**Answer: A. Hardness ions are traded for sodium on the resin**

The resin holds sodium ions loosely and releases them in exchange for calcium and magnesium, so the hardness stays on the resin.

### 47: Lime, soda ash and ion exchange softening (medium)

How is exhausted ion exchange resin restored?

- **A.** By washing it with a lime slurry
- **B.** By flushing it with strong brine
- **C.** By drying it in warm air
- **D.** By soaking it in chlorinated water

**Answer: B. By flushing it with strong brine**

Brine floods the resin with sodium, which pushes the held calcium and magnesium off and recharges the resin for the next cycle.

### 48: Lime, soda ash and ion exchange softening (medium)

Why is carbon dioxide often added after lime softening?

- **A.** To raise hardness back up to a target level
- **B.** To make floc settle faster in the basin
- **C.** To disinfect the water before the clearwell
- **D.** To bring the high pH back down

**Answer: D. To bring the high pH back down**

Lime softened water leaves at a high pH and is prone to deposit calcium carbonate on filter media and pipes. Carbon dioxide lowers the pH and reduces this tendency.

### 49: Lime, soda ash and ion exchange softening (hard)

A plant using lime must remove magnesium hardness as well as calcium hardness. How does the pH it needs compare with removing calcium alone?

- **A.** A lower pH is needed, since magnesium dissolves more easily in acid
- **B.** No pH change is needed, since soda ash handles magnesium
- **C.** The same pH is needed, since both settle as carbonates
- **D.** A higher pH is needed, since magnesium settles as a hydroxide

**Answer: D. A higher pH is needed, since magnesium settles as a hydroxide**

Calcium carbonate precipitates at a moderately high pH, while magnesium hydroxide needs a clearly higher pH, so removing magnesium takes extra lime.

### 50: pH, alkalinity, inhibitors and lead and copper (easy)

Which water is most likely to attack metal pipes?

- **A.** Water carrying a protective phosphate film
- **B.** Soft water with low pH and little alkalinity
- **C.** Water that already tends to deposit calcium carbonate scale
- **D.** Hard water with a pH above neutral and ample alkalinity

**Answer: B. Soft water with low pH and little alkalinity**

Soft, acidic water with little buffering tends to dissolve metal and protective scale. The other waters protect the pipe surface.

### 51: pH, alkalinity, inhibitors and lead and copper (easy)

What do orthophosphate-type corrosion inhibitors aim to do?

- **A.** Coat the pipe wall with a thin layer
- **B.** Raise hardness so scale builds up quickly
- **C.** Kill bacteria growing on the pipe wall
- **D.** Remove dissolved metals at the source

**Answer: A. Coat the pipe wall with a thin layer**

The inhibitor reacts with metal surfaces to form a coating that reduces how much metal enters the water.

### 52: pH, alkalinity, inhibitors and lead and copper (medium)

How do lead and copper usually get into tap water?

- **A.** By arriving in the raw water source in most supplies
- **B.** By forming as the disinfectant breaks down
- **C.** By dissolving from pipes and fixtures
- **D.** By being carried in with the coagulant

**Answer: C. By dissolving from pipes and fixtures**

Those metals are seldom present in source water. Corrosion of pipe, solder and fixtures releases them, so treatment aims to make the water less aggressive.

### 53: pH, alkalinity, inhibitors and lead and copper (medium)

What does raising the pH of a soft, corrosive water toward the slightly alkaline range generally do?

- **B.** Cuts the amount of metal dissolving
- **C.** Raises the rate of metal loss from the pipe
- **D.** Drops the alkalinity to near zero

**Answer: B. Cuts the amount of metal dissolving**

Moving pH upward generally helps protective scale form and lowers metal solubility, which is why pH adjustment is a common corrosion control step.

### 54: pH, alkalinity, inhibitors and lead and copper (medium)

A water has a negative Langelier saturation index. How is it described?

- **A.** Undersaturated with calcium carbonate and corrosive
- **B.** Neutral in pH, so it has no effect on pipes
- **C.** Oversaturated with calcium carbonate, so it tends to form scale
- **D.** Saturated with calcium carbonate, so it is stable

**Answer: A. Undersaturated with calcium carbonate and corrosive**

A negative index means the water would dissolve calcium carbonate rather than deposit it, so it is unlikely to build protective scale and may attack pipe.

### 55: pH, alkalinity, inhibitors and lead and copper (medium)
