Which raw water source usually shows the biggest jump in turbidity after a heavy rainstorm?|A river draining open land|Water from a deep confined aquifer protected by clay layers|Water from a well with a long steel casing|Water held in a covered finished-water tank|Surface runoff carries soil and debris into rivers quickly, while deep groundwater is filtered by the ground and changes slowly.|e
What does turbidity tell an operator about a raw water sample?|How cloudy it is from suspended particles|How much dissolved calcium and magnesium it holds in solution|How well it resists a change in pH when acid is added|How much dissolved gas it carries at the sample temperature|Turbidity is a measure of light scattered by suspended particles such as clay, silt and organisms. Dissolved minerals and buffering are different properties.|e
In water treatment, what does alkalinity describe?|Resistance to a drop in pH when acid is added|The amount of suspended clay and silt carried by the water|The total of dissolved salts that leave scale on heaters|The quantity of oxygen available to aquatic life in the source|Alkalinity is the buffering capacity of the water, mostly from bicarbonate, carbonate and hydroxide. It matters because coagulants consume it.|e
Which pair of dissolved minerals is mainly responsible for the hardness of a water supply?|Calcium and magnesium|Sodium and potassium found in many brines|Chloride and sulfate from road salt and rock|Iron and manganese from deep wells|Hardness is the sum of the calcium and magnesium concentrations, usually expressed as mg/L as calcium carbonate.|m
A cold winter raw water supply is treated with the same coagulant dose as in summer. What is the usual effect of the low temperature on settling?|Particles settle more slowly because the water is thicker|Particles settle faster because the water is denser than in summer|Particles settle at the same speed because their own weight sets the rate|Floc forms faster so the basins need less time to do the job|Cold water is more viscous, so it resists the fall of particles and slows both floc formation and settling. Plants often adjust dose or mixing in winter.|m
A river intake receives a large rainfall runoff. Which change in the raw water is most likely?|Turbidity climbs and alkalinity often drops|Turbidity falls and hardness climbs because of dilution of solids|Alkalinity climbs and the color of the water disappears|Hardness climbs and the dissolved gases leave the water|Runoff brings in solids and dilutes the mineral content that gives alkalinity and hardness, so coagulant demand can change quickly.|m
Why do operators of a deep reservoir intake watch for the lake turning over in autumn?|Oxygen-poor bottom water mixes upward and may bring in iron, manganese and odors|Cooling surface water strips the dissolved minerals out of the lake|Algae growth stops, so turbidity falls near zero at all depths|Alkalinity is lost from the water as the layers mix together|When temperature layers break down, bottom water rich in dissolved metals and decay products reaches the intake depth, and treatment needs often change.|h

@Aeration|FLHB|Gas transfer and oxidation
What is the main job of aeration in a drinking water plant?|Moving gases between the water and the air|Settling heavy particles out of the water before filtration|Adding hardness to make the water taste better|Leaving a lasting disinfectant residual in the pipes|Aeration brings water and air into close contact so gases can be added, such as oxygen, or stripped, such as hydrogen sulfide and carbon dioxide.|e
Raw well water has a rotten-egg smell. Which gas does aeration help remove?|Hydrogen sulfide|Oxygen from the surrounding air|Nitrogen that is dissolved from the air|Ozone left from earlier treatment|Hydrogen sulfide is a dissolved gas that escapes to the air during aeration, which reduces the odor. Oxygen and nitrogen are gases that normally dissolve into the water.|e
Aeration strips carbon dioxide from a groundwater. What happens to the pH?|It rises as carbonic acid is lost|It falls because oxygen has been added to the water|It stays the same because air is a neutral gas|It falls because alkalinity is removed by the spray|Dissolved carbon dioxide forms a weak acid. When it leaves the water the acid is reduced and the pH goes up.|m
Why does aeration help in removing dissolved iron from a well supply?|Oxygen turns it into solid particles|Air dissolves the iron into a gas that escapes from the water|Air lowers the pH enough to dissolve the iron deposits|Air disinfects the iron-using bacteria that live in the water|Ferrous iron in solution reacts with oxygen and forms ferric iron compounds that do not dissolve, so they can be removed as particles.|m
Why does a spray aerator break water into small droplets?|It gives more water surface for gas transfer|It slows the water so more sludge can settle out|It adds pressure so that gases stay dissolved in the water|It cools the water to lower its hardness before treatment|Gas moves across the water-air surface, so more surface area per volume means faster transfer.|m
Compared with cold water, what is the oxygen saturation level in warm water?|Lower, since gases are less soluble as water warms|Higher, because warm molecules hold more dissolved gas|The same, because air pressure alone sets the value|Higher, because warm water is thinner and lets gas in faster|The solubility of gases drops as temperature rises, so warm water holds less dissolved oxygen at saturation.|m
Brown crusty deposits keep building on the trays and nozzles of an aerator treating raw well water. What is the most likely cause?|Iron and manganese oxidized by the air|Coagulant carried in from an upstream basin and left on the metal|Sludge rising from a clarifier and splashing into the spray|Disinfection byproducts forming as the droplets fall through air|Dissolved metals in the well water react with oxygen and form solid oxides that stick to surfaces, so aerators need routine cleaning.|h
@Coagulation and Flocculation|FLHB|Coagulants, mixing, jar testing and chemistry
What does a coagulant do to the tiny particles in raw water?|Reduces the charge that keeps them apart|Dissolves them so they pass through the filters easily|Raises their weight by adding heavy minerals to each one|Kills them so they stop moving through the water|Small particles carry like charges that keep them apart. A coagulant neutralizes this charge so the particles can stick together.|e
Why is a coagulant added where the water is mixed very vigorously?|It spreads through the water in seconds|It breaks up the floc that has already formed in the basin|It lets sludge settle faster inside the pipe|It raises the temperature of the water before settling|The coagulant reacts almost at once, so it must be dispersed quickly and evenly for good particle contact.|e
What is the purpose of a jar test?|To find a good dose using small samples|To measure how much water a plant treats in a day|To calibrate a turbidimeter before the day's readings|To measure the sludge solids sitting in a basin|A jar test copies mixing and settling on a small scale so an operator can compare doses and pH before changing the plant feed.|e
Why is the water stirred slowly during flocculation instead of vigorously?|Particles meet and grow without breaking apart|The floc dissolves back into the water at higher speeds|Slow stirring raises the coagulant dose needed for treatment|Slow stirring makes the particles repel each other more|Gentle motion brings particles together to build larger floc, while strong shear tears fragile floc into small pieces.|m
What does feeding alum do to raw water alkalinity and pH?|Both fall because alum is acidic|Both rise because alum behaves like a strong base|The pH rises while the alkalinity falls|Both stay the same because alum is a neutral salt|Alum reacts with alkalinity when it forms floc and releases acid, so alkalinity and pH go down.|m
Low-alkalinity raw water cannot hold its pH when alum is fed. What is a common correction?|Add lime or soda ash|Add acid to lower the pH even further for floc growth|Increase the flocculator speed to keep floc suspended|Shut off the rapid mix to save coagulant|A base adds the buffering that alum consumes, so the pH stays in a range where floc forms well.|m
Four jars are given alum at 10, 20, 30 and 40 mg/L. After settling, the turbidities are 5.2, 2.4, 0.8 and 1.9 NTU. What is the best conclusion?|30 mg/L works best and 40 mg/L looks like an overdose|40 mg/L works best because more coagulant makes more floc|20 mg/L is best because it is cheaper and removes most turbidity|10 mg/L is best because the dose is the lowest tested|The lowest settled turbidity shows the best dose. Turbidity rising at the higher dose is a sign of overfeeding, which can restabilize particles.|h

Which description fits floc after good flocculation?|Visible clumps that settle readily|A fine haze that stays suspended for hours in the basin|Dissolved salts that cannot be seen in the water|Gas bubbles that rise to the surface of the tank|Floc is a cluster of particles and coagulant solids large enough to sink in the settling basin.|e
@Clarification and Sedimentation|FLHB|Settling, detention time, overflow rate and sludge
What is the purpose of a sedimentation basin?|To let floc settle out before the filters|To add dissolved oxygen to the water before it is stored|To disinfect the water through long contact time|To raise the alkalinity of the water before filtration|Quiet water with a long path lets suspended solids sink to the floor, which reduces the load on the filters.|e
What does detention time describe for a basin?|The average time water spends in it|The time needed for a jar test to finish a full run|The time between sludge removals from the floor|The time the coagulant takes to dissolve in the feed tank|Detention time is basin volume divided by flow. It shows how long water has to settle on average.|e
What is short-circuiting in a sedimentation basin?|Part of the flow reaches the outlet faster than average|Water recirculates in the basin and gets extra settling|Sludge collects in a single corner of the floor|Chemicals react before they reach the basin inlet|Density currents, wind or poor baffles can send part of the flow straight to the outlet, so real settling time is less than calculated.|m
Floc is seen carrying over the effluent weirs of a clarifier. Which cause fits best?|Flow per foot of weir is too high|The sludge blanket has compacted too much on the floor|The detention time is longer than the design value|The water is warmer than the design temperature|High weir loading makes strong upward currents near the outlet that can lift light floc into the settled water.|m
Why should settled sludge be removed on a regular schedule?|Buildup takes basin volume and can release odors|It lowers the detention time to zero at the weirs|It raises the alkalinity of the water leaving the basin|It makes the coagulant feed less efficient at night|Buildup shrinks the working volume, can resuspend solids and can decay and cause problems in the finished water.|m
How do tube or plate settlers improve clarifier performance?|They add settling surface so particles fall a short distance|They add oxygen to speed up floc formation in the basin|They remove sludge by suction at the outlet weir|They raise the temperature of the water to settle faster|The shallow channels give particles only a short fall to a surface, so the basin can handle more flow or give better clarity.|m
Flow to a rectangular settling basin doubles and nothing else changes. What happens?|Overflow rate doubles and fine floc may escape|Overflow rate halves and settling improves for small floc|Overflow rate stays the same since the area is unchanged|Overflow rate doubles and detention time also doubles|Overflow rate is flow divided by surface area, so doubling the flow doubles it, and detention time halves, so finer floc may carry over.|h
