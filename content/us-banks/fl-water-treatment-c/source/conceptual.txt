@Source Water|FLHB|Raw water quality and seasonal change
Which raw water source usually shows the biggest jump in turbidity after a heavy rainstorm?|A river or stream draining open farmland|Water from a deep confined aquifer protected by clay layers|Water from a well with a long steel casing|Water held in a covered finished-water tank|Surface runoff carries soil and debris into rivers quickly, while deep groundwater is filtered by the ground and changes slowly.|e
What does turbidity tell an operator about a raw water sample?|How cloudy it is from suspended particles|How much dissolved calcium and magnesium it holds in solution|How well it resists a change in pH when acid is added|How much dissolved gas it carries at the sample temperature|Turbidity is a measure of light scattered by suspended particles such as clay, silt and organisms. Dissolved minerals and buffering are different properties.|e
In water treatment, what does alkalinity describe?|Resistance to a drop in pH when acid is added to it|The amount of suspended clay and silt carried by the water|The total of dissolved salts that leave scale on heaters|The quantity of oxygen available to aquatic life in the source|Alkalinity is the buffering capacity of the water, mostly from bicarbonate, carbonate and hydroxide. It matters because coagulants consume it.|e
Which pair of dissolved minerals is mainly responsible for the hardness of a water supply?|Calcium and magnesium dissolved from rock|Sodium and potassium found in many brines|Chloride and sulfate from road salt and rock|Iron and manganese from deep wells|Hardness is the sum of the calcium and magnesium concentrations, usually expressed as mg/L as calcium carbonate.|m
A cold winter raw water supply is treated with the same coagulant dose as in summer. What is the usual effect of the low temperature on settling?|Particles settle more slowly because the water is more viscous|Particles settle faster because the water is denser than in summer|Particles settle at the same speed because their own weight sets the rate|Floc forms faster so the basins need less time to do the job|Cold water is more viscous, so it resists the fall of particles and slows both floc formation and settling. Plants often adjust dose or mixing in winter.|m
A river intake receives a large rainfall runoff. Which change in the raw water is most likely?|Turbidity climbs and alkalinity often drops from dilution|Turbidity falls and hardness climbs because of dilution of solids|Alkalinity climbs and the color of the water disappears|Hardness climbs and the dissolved gases leave the water|Runoff brings in solids and dilutes the mineral content that gives alkalinity and hardness, so coagulant demand can change quickly.|m
Why do operators of a deep reservoir intake watch for the lake turning over in autumn?|Bottom water mixes upward and may bring iron, manganese and odors|Cooling surface water strips dissolved minerals out of the lake|Algae growth stops, so turbidity falls near zero at all depths|Alkalinity is lost from the water as the layers mix together|When temperature layers break down, bottom water rich in dissolved metals and decay products reaches the intake depth, and treatment needs often change.|h
@Aeration|FLHB|Gas transfer and oxidation
What is the main job of aeration in a drinking water plant?|Moving gases between the water and the air|Settling heavy particles out of the water before filtration|Adding hardness to make the water taste better|Leaving a lasting disinfectant residual in the pipes|Aeration brings water and air into close contact so gases can be added, such as oxygen, or stripped, such as hydrogen sulfide and carbon dioxide.|e
Raw well water has a rotten-egg smell. Which gas does aeration help remove?|Hydrogen sulfide gas from decaying matter|Oxygen from the surrounding air|Nitrogen that is dissolved from the air|Ozone left from earlier treatment|Hydrogen sulfide is a dissolved gas that escapes to the air during aeration, which reduces the odor. Oxygen and nitrogen are gases that normally dissolve into the water.|e
Aeration strips carbon dioxide from a groundwater. What happens to the pH?|It rises as carbonic acid is lost from the water|It falls because oxygen has been added to the water|It stays the same because air is a neutral gas|It falls because alkalinity is removed by the spray|Dissolved carbon dioxide forms a weak acid. When it leaves the water the acid is reduced and the pH goes up.|m
Why does aeration help in removing dissolved iron from a well supply?|Oxygen turns it into solid particles that can be removed|Air dissolves the iron into a gas that escapes from the water|Air lowers the pH enough to dissolve the iron deposits|Air disinfects the iron-using bacteria that live in the water|Ferrous iron in solution reacts with oxygen and forms ferric iron compounds that do not dissolve, so they can be removed as particles.|m
Why does a spray aerator break water into small droplets?|It gives more water surface area for faster gas transfer|It slows the water so more sludge can settle out|It adds pressure so that gases stay dissolved in the water|It cools the water to lower its hardness before treatment|Gas moves across the water-air surface, so more surface area per volume means faster transfer.|m
Compared with cold water, what is the oxygen saturation level in warm water?|Lower, since gases are less soluble as the water warms up|Higher, because warm molecules hold more dissolved gas|The same, because air pressure alone sets the value|Higher, because warm water is thinner and lets gas in faster|The solubility of gases drops as temperature rises, so warm water holds less dissolved oxygen at saturation.|m
Brown crusty deposits keep building on the trays and nozzles of an aerator treating raw well water. What is the most likely cause?|Iron and manganese oxidized by the air and left as solids|Coagulant carried in from an upstream basin and left on the metal|Sludge rising from a clarifier and splashing into the spray|Disinfection byproducts forming as the droplets fall through air|Dissolved metals in the well water react with oxygen and form solid oxides that stick to surfaces, so aerators need routine cleaning.|h
@Coagulation and Flocculation|FLHB|Coagulants, mixing, jar testing and chemistry
What does a coagulant do to the tiny particles in raw water?|Reduces the charge that keeps them apart in the water|Dissolves them so they pass through the filters easily|Raises their weight by adding heavy minerals to each one|Kills them so they stop moving through the water|Small particles carry like charges that keep them apart. A coagulant neutralizes this charge so the particles can stick together.|e
Why is a coagulant added where the water is mixed very vigorously?|It spreads through the water evenly within seconds|It breaks up the floc that has already formed in the basin|It lets sludge settle faster inside the pipe|It raises the temperature of the water before settling|The coagulant reacts almost at once, so it must be dispersed quickly and evenly for good particle contact.|e
What is the purpose of a jar test?|To find a good dose using small samples of raw water|To measure how much water a plant treats in a day|To calibrate a turbidimeter before the day's readings|To measure the sludge solids sitting in a basin|A jar test copies mixing and settling on a small scale so an operator can compare doses and pH before changing the plant feed.|e
Why is the water stirred slowly during flocculation instead of vigorously?|Particles meet and grow without being torn apart|The floc dissolves back into the water at higher speeds|Slow stirring raises the coagulant dose needed for treatment|Slow stirring makes the particles repel each other more|Gentle motion brings particles together to build larger floc, while strong shear tears fragile floc into small pieces.|m
What does feeding alum do to raw water alkalinity and pH?|Both fall because alum is acidic and uses up alkalinity|Both rise because alum behaves like a strong base|The pH rises while the alkalinity falls|Both stay the same because alum is a neutral salt|Alum reacts with alkalinity when it forms floc and releases acid, so alkalinity and pH go down.|m
Low-alkalinity raw water cannot hold its pH when alum is fed. What is a common correction?|Add lime or soda ash to supply alkalinity|Add acid to lower the pH even further for floc growth|Increase the flocculator speed to keep floc suspended|Shut off the rapid mix to save coagulant|A base adds the buffering that alum consumes, so the pH stays in a range where floc forms well.|m
Four jars are given alum at 10, 20, 30 and 40 mg/L. After settling, the turbidities are 5.2, 2.4, 0.8 and 1.9 NTU. What is the best conclusion?|30 mg/L works best, and 40 mg/L looks like an overdose|40 mg/L works best because more coagulant makes more floc|20 mg/L is best because it is cheaper and removes most turbidity|10 mg/L is best because the dose is the lowest tested|The lowest settled turbidity shows the best dose. Turbidity rising at the higher dose is a sign of overfeeding, which can restabilize particles.|h
@Clarification and Sedimentation|FLHB|Settling, detention time, overflow rate and sludge
What is the purpose of a sedimentation basin?|To let floc settle out by gravity before the filters|To add dissolved oxygen to the water before it is stored|To disinfect the water through long contact time|To raise the alkalinity of the water before filtration|Quiet water with a long path lets suspended solids sink to the floor, which reduces the load on the filters.|e
What does detention time describe for a basin?|The average time water spends in the basin|The time needed for a jar test to finish a full run|The time between sludge removals from the floor|The time the coagulant takes to dissolve in the feed tank|Detention time is basin volume divided by flow. It shows how long water has to settle on average.|e
What is short-circuiting in a sedimentation basin?|Part of the flow reaches the outlet faster than the average|Water recirculates in the basin and gets extra settling|Sludge collects in a single corner of the floor|Chemicals react before they reach the basin inlet|Density currents, wind or poor baffles can send part of the flow straight to the outlet, so real settling time is less than calculated.|m
Floc is seen carrying over the effluent weirs of a clarifier. Which cause fits best?|Flow per foot of weir is too high for the design|The sludge blanket has compacted too much on the floor|The detention time is longer than the design value|The water is warmer than the design temperature|High weir loading makes strong upward currents near the outlet that can lift light floc into the settled water.|m
Why should settled sludge be removed on a regular schedule?|Buildup takes basin volume and can release odors later|It lowers the detention time to zero at the weirs|It raises the alkalinity of the water leaving the basin|It makes the coagulant feed less efficient at night|Buildup shrinks the working volume, can resuspend solids and can decay and cause problems in the finished water.|m
How do tube or plate settlers improve clarifier performance?|They add settling surface so particles fall a short distance|They add oxygen to speed up floc formation in the basin|They remove sludge by suction at the outlet weir|They raise the temperature of the water to settle faster|The shallow channels give particles only a short fall to a surface, so the basin can handle more flow or give better clarity.|m
Flow to a rectangular settling basin doubles and nothing else changes. What happens?|Overflow rate doubles and fine floc may escape the basin|Overflow rate halves and settling improves for small floc|Overflow rate stays the same since the area is unchanged|Overflow rate doubles and detention time also doubles|Overflow rate is flow divided by surface area, so doubling the flow doubles it, and detention time halves, so finer floc may carry over.|h
@Filtration|FLHB|Rapid sand, backwash, slow sand and membranes
What is the main job of a rapid sand filter in a surface water plant?|Catch fine particles left after settling|Dissolve calcium and magnesium out of the water|Leave a chemical residual to protect the pipes|Strip dissolved gases from the water before storage|Granular media traps small floc and particles that escape the sedimentation basin, giving clear water. It does not change hardness, residual or gas content.|e
Why is a filter backwashed?|To wash trapped solids out of the media bed|To add new media grains to the top of the bed|To raise the pH of the water before the clearwell|To measure the rate of flow through the bed|Solids lodged between the grains raise head loss. Reversing the flow lifts and scrubs them away so the filter can run again.|e
During a filter run, the head loss across the bed slowly climbs. What is the usual reason?|Trapped solids fill the pores in the bed|The media grains swell as they take up water|The water gets warmer as it moves down the bed|The underdrain openings widen with use|As solids collect in the pore spaces, water meets more resistance and more pressure is lost. A rising head loss is a signal that a wash is coming due.|m
Why is a filter often run to waste for a short time after a backwash?|Early filtrate may be cloudy, so it is diverted|The wash trough has to drain before the bed is used again|Chlorine must be rinsed off the media before use|Head loss has to be built up to a working value|Right after cleaning, the bed has not yet regained good particle capture, so the early water is sent away instead of to the clearwell.|m
What does a slow sand filter depend on for much of its cleaning action?|A living biological layer at the top of the sand|Strong backwash flows that lift the whole bed|Coagulant fed directly onto the bed surface|A high rate of flow that forces particles through|Slow sand filters run at low rates with no chemical feed. A biologically active layer on the surface traps particles and breaks down organisms.|m
How do low-pressure membrane filters mainly remove particles from water?|By blocking particles larger than the pores|By trapping them on a sticky biological film|By reacting them with a coagulant on the surface|By letting them settle in quiet channels|Membrane filtration works like a physical sieve, and the pore size sets what is blocked. It does not rely on settling, biology or chemical reaction.|m
A filter shows rising effluent turbidity soon after it returns to service, yet its head loss is still low. Which cause fits best?|Weak floc from poor coagulation slips through|Mudballs have formed and blocked the bed|The filter has reached its limit of head loss|The backwash flow was too gentle to lift the bed|Particles slipping through while head loss stays low point to poor pretreatment that makes weak floc. Blocked media or a spent run would show high head loss.|h
@Disinfection and Disinfection Byproducts|FLHB|Chlorine, contact time, residual and byproducts
What is a disinfectant residual?|The disinfectant still present after the contact period|The amount of chemical measured out by the feed pump|The portion of the dose used up by reacting with impurities|The chemical left in the supply cylinder after a feed|Operators measure what is left in the water to confirm protection carries beyond the plant. The portion that gets used up is the demand, not the residual.|e
Chlorine gas escapes from a leak into a room. Where does it tend to collect?|In low areas, since it is denser than air|Near the ceiling, since it is lighter than air|Evenly through the room, since gases mix at once|Around the windows, since it is drawn to light|Chlorine gas is about two and a half times as dense as air, so a release settles toward the floor and into low spots.|e
In disinfection, what does the term CT mean?|Residual concentration times contact time|Chemical temperature multiplied by water pH|Chlorine tank capacity divided by plant flow|Coagulant turbidity ratio across the filter|CT combines how much disinfectant is in the water with how long the water stays in contact. A larger CT gives more inactivation of organisms.|m
Why are baffles built into a chlorine contact basin?|They stop part of the flow rushing through early|They raise the chlorine concentration inside the basin|They warm the water to speed up the reaction|They remove ammonia before the water leaves|Baffles force the water along a longer path, so less of it passes through early and the effective contact time moves closer to the calculated time.|m
As pH rises, what happens to the killing power of free chlorine?|It weakens because less hypochlorous acid is present|It strengthens because more hypochlorite ion is present|It stays the same because the dose has not changed|It strengthens because more chlorine gas is released|Hypochlorous acid is the stronger disinfectant form of free chlorine, and it shifts toward the weaker hypochlorite ion as pH goes up.|m
Which step best lowers the formation of trihalomethanes and haloacetic acids?|Removing natural organic matter ahead of chlorine|Feeding chlorine at the plant inlet at a higher dose|Letting treated water sit longer in the contact basin|Warming the water in the clearwell|These byproducts form when chlorine reacts with natural organic matter, so taking that material out first leaves less to react. More chlorine, time and warmth tend to increase formation.|m
Chlorine is added in rising doses to water that contains ammonia. The residual climbs, dips, then climbs again. What does the dip show?|Combined chlorine is largely broken down|The demand from the filter media has been met|Combined chlorine is just starting to form|Chlorine is leaving the water as gas and wasting dose|At the low point, chloramines are being broken down by the added chlorine. Doses beyond that leave a free chlorine residual that rises with the dose.|h
@Softening|FLHB|Lime, soda ash and ion exchange softening
What is the goal of precipitation softening?|Turn hardness minerals into removable solids|Add calcium and magnesium to the water for taste|Swap sodium for hardness minerals on a resin bed|Lower the pH so scale dissolves from the pipes|Chemicals such as lime change the water chemistry so that hardness minerals form solids, which settle or are filtered out.|e
Which is a common sign of very hard water in a home?|Scale on heaters and poor soap lather|A rotten-egg odor at the hot tap|A cloudy look that clears from the bottom up|A bitter taste that develops after boiling|Calcium and magnesium leave scale when water is heated and react with soap, which cuts the lather. The other signs point to hydrogen sulfide or trapped air.|e
Which kind of hardness needs soda ash in addition to lime?|Noncarbonate hardness, not tied to alkalinity|Carbonate hardness, which lime alone can remove|Hardness caused by sodium in the raw water|Hardness that disappears when the water is boiled|Lime removes carbonate hardness. Soda ash supplies carbonate that precipitates the calcium left behind as noncarbonate hardness.|m
In a cation exchange softener, what happens as hard water passes through the resin?|Hardness ions are traded for sodium on the resin|Calcium and magnesium form solids that settle on top|The resin adds hydroxide that lifts the pH close to 11|The resin takes up the sodium and leaves the hardness|The resin holds sodium ions loosely and releases them in exchange for calcium and magnesium, so the hardness stays on the resin.|m
How is exhausted ion exchange resin restored?|By flushing it with strong brine|By washing it with a lime slurry|By soaking it in chlorinated water|By drying it in warm air|Brine floods the resin with sodium, which pushes the held calcium and magnesium off and recharges the resin for the next cycle.|m
Why is carbon dioxide often added after lime softening?|To bring the high pH back down|To raise hardness back up to a target level|To disinfect the water before the clearwell|To make floc settle faster in the basin|Lime softened water leaves at a high pH and is prone to deposit calcium carbonate on filter media and pipes. Carbon dioxide lowers the pH and reduces this tendency.|m
A plant using lime must remove magnesium hardness as well as calcium hardness. How does the pH it needs compare with removing calcium alone?|A higher pH is needed, since magnesium settles as a hydroxide|A lower pH is needed, since magnesium dissolves more easily in acid|The same pH is needed, since both settle as carbonates|No pH change is needed, since soda ash handles magnesium|Calcium carbonate precipitates at a moderately high pH, while magnesium hydroxide needs a clearly higher pH, so removing magnesium takes extra lime.|h
@Corrosion Control|FLHB|pH, alkalinity, inhibitors and lead and copper
Which water is most likely to attack metal pipes?|Soft water with low pH and little alkalinity|Hard water with a pH above neutral and ample alkalinity|Water that already tends to deposit calcium carbonate scale|Water carrying a protective phosphate film|Soft, acidic water with little buffering tends to dissolve metal and protective scale. What do orthophosphate-type corrosion inhibitors aim to do?|Coat the pipe wall with a thin layer|Raise hardness so scale builds up quickly|Kill bacteria growing on the pipe wall|Remove dissolved metals at the source|The inhibitor reacts with metal surfaces to form a coating that reduces how much metal enters the water.|e
How do lead and copper usually get into tap water?|By dissolving from pipes and fixtures|By arriving in the raw water source in most supplies|By being carried in with the coagulant|By forming as the disinfectant breaks down|Those metals are seldom present in source water. What does raising the pH of a soft, corrosive water toward the slightly alkaline range generally do?|Cuts the amount of metal dissolving|Raises the rate of metal loss from the pipe|Strips the disinfectant residual from the water|Drops the alkalinity to near zero|Moving pH upward generally helps protective scale form and lowers metal solubility, which is why pH adjustment is a common corrosion control step.|m

Which result is most plausible?|Weaker buffering can let pipe scale dissolve|Corrosion falls since the water has less dissolved mineral content|There is no effect, since pH alone decides corrosion|Scale grows faster since calcium carbonate drops out|Lower alkalinity makes pH less stable and the water more aggressive toward protective scale, so metal release can rise even when pH looks similar.|h
