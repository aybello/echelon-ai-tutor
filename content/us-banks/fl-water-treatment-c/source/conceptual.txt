@Source Water|FLHB|Raw water quality and seasonal change
Which raw water source usually shows the biggest jump in turbidity after a heavy rainstorm?|A river or stream that drains open land|Water from a deep confined aquifer|Water from a well with a long casing|Water stored in a covered finished-water tank|Surface runoff carries soil and debris into rivers quickly, while deep groundwater is filtered by the ground and changes slowly.|e
What does turbidity tell an operator about a raw water sample?|How cloudy it is because of suspended particles|How much dissolved calcium and magnesium it holds|How well it resists a change in pH|How much dissolved gas it carries|Turbidity is a measure of light scattered by suspended particles such as clay, silt and organisms. Dissolved minerals and buffering are different properties.|e
In water treatment, what does alkalinity describe?|The ability of the water to resist a drop in pH when acid is added|The amount of suspended clay and silt in the water|The total of dissolved salts that leave scale on heaters|The quantity of oxygen available to aquatic life|Alkalinity is the buffering capacity of the water, mostly from bicarbonate, carbonate and hydroxide. It matters because coagulants consume it.|e
Which pair of dissolved minerals is mainly responsible for the hardness of a water supply?|Calcium and magnesium|Sodium and potassium|Chloride and sulfate|Iron and manganese|Hardness is the sum of the calcium and magnesium concentrations, usually expressed as mg/L as calcium carbonate.|m
A cold winter raw water supply is treated with the same coagulant dose as in summer. What is the usual effect of the low temperature on settling?|Particles settle more slowly because the water is more viscous|Particles settle faster because the water is denser|Particles settle at the same speed because their weight sets the rate|Floc forms faster so basins need less time|Cold water is thicker, so it resists the fall of particles and slows both floc formation and settling. Plants often adjust dose or mixing in winter.|m
A river intake receives a large rainfall runoff. Which change in the raw water is most likely?|Turbidity climbs and alkalinity often drops from dilution|Turbidity falls and hardness climbs|Alkalinity climbs and color disappears|Hardness climbs and dissolved gases vanish|Runoff brings in solids and dilutes the mineral content that gives alkalinity and hardness, so coagulant demand can change quickly.|m
Why do operators of a deep reservoir intake watch for the lake turning over in autumn?|Oxygen-poor bottom water mixes upward and can bring iron, manganese and odor-causing material to the intake|Cooling surface water carries away all dissolved minerals from the lake|Algae growth stops, so turbidity falls near zero at all depths|Alkalinity is lost from the water as it mixes|When temperature layers break down, bottom water rich in dissolved metals and decay products reaches the intake depth, and treatment needs often change.|h
@Aeration|FLHB|Gas transfer and oxidation
What is the main job of aeration in a drinking water plant?|Moving gases between the water and the air|Settling heavy particles out of the water|Adding hardness to soften taste|Leaving a lasting disinfectant residual|Aeration brings water and air into close contact so gases can be added, such as oxygen, or stripped, such as hydrogen sulfide and carbon dioxide.|e
Raw well water has a rotten-egg smell. Which gas does aeration help remove?|Hydrogen sulfide|Oxygen|Nitrogen|Ozone|Hydrogen sulfide is a dissolved gas that escapes to the air during aeration, which reduces the odor. Oxygen and nitrogen are gases that normally dissolve into the water.|e
Aeration strips carbon dioxide from a groundwater. What happens to the pH?|It rises because carbonic acid is lost|It falls because oxygen is added|It stays the same because air is neutral|It falls because alkalinity is removed|Dissolved carbon dioxide forms a weak acid. When it leaves the water the acid is reduced and the pH goes up.|m
Why does aeration help in removing dissolved iron from a well supply?|Oxygen converts dissolved iron into solid particles that can be filtered or settled|Air dissolves the iron into a gas that escapes|Air lowers the pH enough to dissolve iron deposits|Air disinfects the iron-bearing bacteria in the water|Ferrous iron in solution reacts with oxygen and forms ferric iron compounds that do not dissolve, so they can be removed as particles.|m
Why does a spray aerator break water into small droplets?|It creates more water surface for faster gas transfer|It slows the water so more sludge settles|It adds pressure to keep gases dissolved|It cools the water to lower its hardness|Gas moves across the water-air surface, so more surface area per volume means faster transfer.|m
Compared with cold water, what is the oxygen saturation level in warm water?|Lower, because gases are less soluble as water warms|Higher, because warm molecules hold more gas|The same, because air pressure sets the value|Higher, because warm water has less viscosity|The solubility of gases drops as temperature rises, so warm water holds less dissolved oxygen at saturation.|m
Brown crusty deposits keep building on the trays and nozzles of an aerator treating raw well water. What is the most likely cause?|Iron and manganese oxidized by the air and depositing as solids|Coagulant carried in from an upstream basin|Sludge rising from a clarifier|Disinfection byproducts forming in the spray|Dissolved metals in the well water react with oxygen and form solid oxides that stick to surfaces, so aerators need routine cleaning.|h
@Coagulation and Flocculation|FLHB|Coagulants, mixing, jar testing and chemistry
What does a coagulant do to the tiny particles in raw water?|Reduces their repelling charge so they can clump|Dissolves them so they pass through filters|Raises their weight by adding minerals|Kills them so they stop moving|Small particles carry like charges that keep them apart. A coagulant neutralizes this charge so the particles can stick together.|e
Why is a coagulant added where the water is mixed very vigorously?|To spread it through the water in a few seconds before reactions finish|To break up floc that has already formed|To let sludge settle faster in the pipe|To raise the water temperature|The coagulant reacts almost at once, so it must be dispersed quickly and evenly for good particle contact.|e
What is the purpose of a jar test?|To find a good coagulant dose and conditions using small samples of raw water|To measure how much water a plant treats in a day|To calibrate a turbidimeter before use|To measure the sludge solids in a basin|A jar test copies mixing and settling on a small scale so an operator can compare doses and pH before changing the plant feed.|e
Why is the water stirred slowly during flocculation instead of vigorously?|Slow stirring lets particles meet and grow without breaking apart|Slow stirring dissolves the floc into the water|Slow stirring raises the coagulant dose needed|Slow stirring makes particles repel each other|Gentle motion brings particles together to build larger floc, while strong shear tears fragile floc into small pieces.|m
What does feeding alum do to raw water alkalinity and pH?|Both fall because alum is acidic and uses up alkalinity|Both rise because alum is a base|The pH rises while alkalinity falls|Both stay the same since alum is a salt|Alum reacts with alkalinity when it forms floc and releases acid, so alkalinity and pH go down.|m
Low-alkalinity raw water cannot hold its pH when alum is fed. What is a common correction?|Add a base such as lime or soda ash to supply alkalinity|Add acid to lower the pH further|Increase the flocculator speed|Shut off the rapid mix|A base adds the buffering that alum consumes, so the pH stays in a range where floc forms well.|m
Four jars are given alum at 10, 20, 30 and 40 mg/L. After settling, the turbidities are 5.2, 2.4, 0.8 and 1.9 NTU. What is the best conclusion?|30 mg/L works best, and the worse result at 40 suggests an overdose|40 mg/L works best because more coagulant makes more floc|20 mg/L is best because it is cheaper and removes most turbidity|10 mg/L is best because the dose is lowest|The lowest settled turbidity shows the best dose. Turbidity rising at the higher dose is a sign of overfeeding, which can restabilize particles.|h
@Clarification and Sedimentation|FLHB|Settling, detention time, overflow rate and sludge
What is the purpose of a sedimentation basin?|To let floc and heavy particles settle out by gravity before filtration|To add dissolved oxygen to the water|To disinfect the water with contact time|To raise the alkalinity of the water|Quiet water with a long path lets suspended solids sink to the floor, which reduces the load on the filters.|e
What does detention time describe for a basin?|The average time a volume of water spends in the basin|The time needed for a jar test to finish|The time between sludge removals|The time the coagulant takes to dissolve|Detention time is basin volume divided by flow. It shows how long water has to settle on average.|e
What is short-circuiting in a sedimentation basin?|Some water moves through faster than average and gets less settling|Water recirculates and gets more settling|Sludge collects in only one corner|Chemicals react before they reach the basin|Density currents, wind or poor baffles can send part of the flow straight to the outlet, so real settling time is less than calculated.|m
Floc is seen carrying over the effluent weirs of a clarifier. Which cause fits best?|The flow per foot of weir is too high and it pulls light floc up|The sludge blanket has compacted too much|The detention time is longer than needed|The water is warmer than design|High weir loading makes strong upward currents near the outlet that can lift light floc into the settled water.|m
Why should settled sludge be removed on a regular schedule?|Left in place it takes up volume and can rise or release taste and odor material|It lowers the detention time to zero|It raises the alkalinity of the water|It makes coagulant feed less efficient only at night|Buildup shrinks the working volume, can resuspend solids and can decay and cause problems in the finished water.|m
How do tube or plate settlers improve clarifier performance?|They add settling surface so particles fall a shorter distance before landing|They add oxygen to speed floc formation|They remove sludge by suction at the outlet|They raise the temperature of the water|The shallow channels give particles only a short fall to a surface, so the basin can handle more flow or give better clarity.|m
Flow to a rectangular settling basin doubles and nothing else changes. What happens?|Overflow rate doubles and smaller floc is more likely to escape|Overflow rate halves and settling improves|Overflow rate stays the same since area is unchanged|Overflow rate doubles and detention time also doubles|Overflow rate is flow divided by surface area, so doubling the flow doubles it, and detention time halves, so finer floc may carry over.|h
